# Read the insights

What a form's answers add up to — how many people finished, where the rest gave up, what they chose
and what they wrote — without opening a single response.

## Before you start

- A published form that has collected some responses. A form with none still appears, and says so:
  **No responses yet**.
- One decision, made before you look: what you would do differently depending on what the numbers
  say. These panels answer questions; they do not ask them for you.

## Open the insights

1. Open **Responses & Analytics** from the **Forms** card on your MemberJunction home page.
2. Pick the form from the list down the left-hand side.
3. Leave the view on **Insights**, which is where it opens. The other half of that switch,
   **Responses**, is the list of individual responses and is the same list as the builder's
   **Responses** tab.
4. Scroll. The sections come in the order the question is usually asked: how many, where they
   stopped, who they were, what they chose, what they wrote.

## If you see a Sample data badge

A blue **Sample data** badge above the report means what it says: the figures below are invented, so
that the page has something to show before a real response exists. Its tooltip is the whole rule —
**Showing seeded sample data until live responses exist**.

Do not read anything into a report carrying that badge, and do not screenshot one for a colleague.
The first genuine response replaces it.

## The numbers at the top

A single large count of responses, and — when anybody started your form and did not finish — a
second line saying how many **started but unfinished**.

Under it, **Completion** splits everyone who started into **Completed** and
**Started, not finished**. A form nobody has opened reads **Nobody has started this form yet.**

Then two facts side by side. **Typical time to complete** is the middle of your respondents, not the
average, so one person who left the tab open over lunch does not drag it. **Last response** is
written as an age rather than a date — *2 hours ago* answers *is this form still collecting?*, and a
date makes you do that subtraction yourself.

## Where people stop

*Of everyone who opened the form, how far did they get?*

One bar per page, longest first, each reading how many **reached** it, with the share who
**left here** between one bar and the next. This is the one section that counts the people who did
not finish — that is the entire point of it, and it is the only place in MJ Forms where those people
are visible individually enough to act on.

A single-page form has nowhere to drop off, and says so:
**This form has a single page, so there is no drop-off to chart.** If your form is long and this
section is missing, it is because everything is on one page — which is itself worth knowing.

## Who responded

*What kind of people are these?*

Counted from the contact questions only — the section's own line says
**Counted from the contact questions. Individual details stay under Responses.**

**Distinct people** is the count of separate email addresses, which is not the same as the count of
responses: a form linked from a newsletter collects the same person twice, and every per-person rate
on the page is wrong if nobody says so. **Work addresses** is the share of those addresses that are
not a well-known consumer mailbox.

Below the numbers, up to three breakdowns: **Email domains**, **Countries** and **Organisations**.

Read **Organisations** with its caveat in hand, because the panel prints it:
**As typed by respondents, so spellings are not merged.** *Acme*, *Acme Ltd* and *acme* are three
organisations on this chart and one organisation in life. It is a rough shape of who answered, never
a count of companies.

## What they chose

*Which options won?*

Every question with an answer you can add up, in the order it is asked. Multiple choice, checkboxes,
dropdowns, ratings, yes/no. **Show all** expands a long list of options and **Show fewer** puts it
back.

A rating or a number question adds **Average**, **Lowest** and **Highest**. A Net Promoter question
gets its own headline with the scale spelled out underneath —
**Net promoter score, −100 to 100** — because that number is not a percentage and is routinely read
as one.

Every rate on this view divides by the number of responses, not by the number of people who answered
that particular question. So a question half your respondents skipped reports 50%, rather than
reporting 100% of the people who bothered. A question nobody has reached yet reads
**No answers yet.**

## What they wrote

*What did people say in their own words?*

One card per open-ended question, with how many **answered**, how many **skipped**, and how long a
typical answer ran. Under that, the words that came up most often across the answers.

*Read this section's caveat literally.* It prints under every list of recurring words:

> **Common English words are filtered out. A starting point for reading, not a summary.**

That is not modesty. Counting words is not reading them: *not recommend* and *would recommend* both
contribute *recommend*, sarcasm counts as praise, and a single furious respondent who repeats
themselves outweighs ten calm ones. Use the list to decide which answers to open, then open them —
the button under each card takes you straight to them.

Two more sentences the panel writes, both of which mean something specific:

- **Short-answer fields often hold names and reference codes, so recurring words are not extracted
  from them.** MJ Forms is declining to pull themes out of a column of surnames.
- **No word appears in more than one answer, so there is no pattern to show yet.** People did
  answer; no two of them used the same word.

A question nobody has answered reads **Nobody has answered this question.**

## Which responses the numbers are made of

Worth holding onto, because it explains figures that otherwise look inconsistent.

Everything above counts *finished* responses — the headline count, the completion split, who
responded, what they chose, what they wrote. Half-finished responses are counted in exactly two
places: the second line under the headline count, and **Where people stop**.

That is deliberate. Drop-off is a fact about the people who abandoned your form, so leaving them out
would measure only the respondents who never dropped off. Everywhere else, mixing them in produces
impossible sentences — *40 of 32 responses gave an email* — on a report that is then worth nothing.

## What happens next

Nothing you do here changes the form or a response; this view only reads. The two moves out of it
are **Responses**, for the individual answers behind a number that surprised you, and
[Export responses](export.md), for the same rows in a spreadsheet.
